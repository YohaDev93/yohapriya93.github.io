ENGINEERING LEADERSHIP

From Senior Developer to Technical Lead: Coding Is No Longer Enough

Lessons from my journey through 13+ years of enterprise software engineering and the shift from implementing solutions to taking broader technical ownership.

By Yohapriya Vairaperumal • Technical Lead • 8 min read

When I started my career as a .NET developer, improving as an engineer largely meant improving my coding skills.

I focused on writing better C#, understanding frameworks, learning SQL, debugging applications and delivering features correctly.

After 13+ years of working on enterprise applications across financial services, renewable energy, healthcare, aviation and HRMS, my perspective has changed significantly.

At Technical Lead level, writing clean and efficient code is still important, but it is only one part of the responsibility.

The question gradually changes from “How should I implement this?” to “What is the right engineering solution for this problem?”

1. Coding is only one part of engineering

As developers, we naturally spend a lot of time thinking about implementation. But a technically correct implementation can still become the wrong solution if it does not consider maintainability, security, performance, deployment complexity or the actual business requirement.

As my responsibilities increased, I began looking beyond an individual feature. I needed to understand how a change affected APIs, databases, integrations, deployments, production support and other engineers working on the system.

Leadership lesson: Good technical decisions optimize for the system and the team, not just the individual piece of code being written today.

2. Architecture decisions need business context

Architecture is not about selecting the most sophisticated technology. It is about selecting an approach that fits the problem.

Before recommending a technical solution, I try to understand questions such as:

  • What business problem are we solving?
  • What scale does the application actually need?
  • What are the security and compliance requirements?
  • How will the system be monitored and supported?
  • How difficult will the solution be for the team to maintain?
  • What happens when an external dependency fails?

Microservices, messaging, caching and cloud services can all be valuable, but introducing complexity without a clear reason can make a system harder to operate rather than better.

3. Production ownership changes your thinking

Supporting production systems has been one of the most valuable parts of my engineering experience.

When you are involved in deployments and production incidents, you naturally start designing differently.

Logging, exception handling, observability, validation, backward compatibility and database performance stop being secondary concerns. They become part of feature development itself.

A feature is not complete simply because it works on a developer machine. It needs to be understandable, deployable, observable and supportable in production.

4. Performance should be measured, not guessed

SQL Server and backend performance optimization have been recurring parts of my work.

One lesson I have learned is not to optimize based purely on assumptions. Start with evidence.

Depending on the problem, that may mean reviewing SQL execution plans, indexes, API timings, application logs, traces or database calls generated by Entity Framework.

Once the actual bottleneck is understood, the solution is usually much more focused and easier to justify.

5. Technical leadership is about enabling the team

Moving into technical leadership also changed how I viewed my own productivity.

As an individual developer, productivity can often be measured by the work you personally complete. As a lead, part of your impact comes from helping other engineers succeed.

Code reviews, mentoring, design discussions and knowledge sharing are therefore not interruptions to engineering work. They are engineering work.

While leading developers, I try to explain why I recommend a particular approach rather than simply prescribing what should be changed.

Leadership lesson: A strong Technical Lead should gradually make the team less dependent on the Technical Lead, not more dependent.

6. Communication becomes a technical skill

Technical leadership requires communicating with developers, QA engineers, product owners, business analysts, clients and other stakeholders.

Each group looks at the same problem differently.

An engineer may care about implementation complexity while a stakeholder may care about delivery risk or business impact. Being able to explain technical trade-offs in the language appropriate for the audience is an important part of senior engineering.

This becomes especially important when requirements are unclear. Clarifying the problem before development begins can prevent far more work than solving misunderstandings after implementation.

7. AI-assisted development is changing how we work

More recently, tools such as GitHub Copilot and Claude have become part of my engineering workflow.

I use AI assistance for activities such as exploring implementation options, understanding unfamiliar code, generating initial test cases, reviewing repetitive code, documentation and accelerating refactoring.

However, AI-generated code still requires engineering judgement.

Security, architecture, performance, maintainability and business correctness cannot simply be delegated to a generated response. The engineer remains responsible for validating what goes into production.

My approach: Use AI to accelerate engineering work, not to outsource engineering responsibility.

8. What changed most for me

The biggest change from Senior Developer to Technical Lead was not a particular framework, cloud service or design pattern.

It was learning to think about a wider system of responsibilities:

  • Is this the right solution for the requirement?
  • Can another developer understand and maintain it?
  • Will it behave reliably in production?
  • Can we diagnose it when something goes wrong?
  • Are we creating unnecessary technical debt?
  • Does the team understand the reasoning behind the design?

Final thoughts

Moving from Senior Developer to Technical Lead was not about writing less code. It was about understanding the broader impact of every technical decision.

I still enjoy being hands-on with .NET, APIs, SQL and cloud technologies. But today I think about those technologies within a larger context: architecture, delivery, production reliability, business requirements and the engineers who will maintain the system after the initial implementation.

Good engineering is not just about making software work today. It is about building systems that teams can understand, maintain, scale and support tomorrow.
